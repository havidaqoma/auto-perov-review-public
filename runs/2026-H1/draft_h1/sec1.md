Recent investigations into interfacial decay in metal halide perovskite solar cells arrive at fundamentally contradictory conclusions regarding the primary driver of non-radiative recombination and operational degradation. While one body of literature attributes performance loss at the buried contact to chemical redox reactions between migrating iodide species and adjacent charge extraction layers, competing reports argue that performance decay is governed entirely by field-driven accumulation of mobile point defects that screen the built-in potential. Disentangling these mechanisms requires operando, spatially resolved transient optoelectronic spectroscopy combined with depth-profiled X-ray photoemission under simultaneous electrical bias and continuous illumination. This unresolved mechanistic conflict reflects a broader transition across the discipline. The principal frontier of perovskite photovoltaics has progressed beyond chasing isolated power conversion efficiency (PCE) records on sub-0.1 cm² laboratory apertures. Instead, the central challenges reside in reproducible third-party verification, maintaining performance across commercially relevant module areas, and ensuring operating lifetime under continuous operational stress.

Across the six months from January to June 2026, 3328 indexed works met the scope gate and 884 were read closely. This body of literature reveals that closing the gap between small-area laboratory devices and commercial modules requires resolving several interrelated physical processes across the device stack. This review follows seven critical mechanistic threads: trap states, passivation and ionic motion; contact chemistry at the buried interface; A-site and halide composition engineering; tandem architecture and current matching; degradation mechanisms under operation; and large-area coating and the area penalty. Defect chemistry cannot be isolated from ionic dynamics, as shallow point defects migrate under operating electric fields, transforming into deep carrier traps or accumulating at transport layers. Similarly, contact chemistry at the buried interface dictates initial crystallization kinetics, internal strain, and energetic alignment, which dictate long-term resistance to ion migration and delamination. Meanwhile, A-site and halide composition engineering directly governs thermodynamic phase stability against segregation under light and thermal stress, setting the operational boundary for tandem architecture and current matching in monolithic multijunctions.

Translating these microscopic stabilization strategies to industrially scalable dimensions exposes the area penalty incurred during large-area coating, where meniscus drying kinetics, fluid dynamics, and substrate thermal gradients induce spatial variations in film stoichiometry and defect density. Under actual operating conditions, these initial microstructural non-uniformities accelerate localized degradation mechanisms under operation, precipitating failure through moisture ingress, heat-induced halide redistribution, and electrode corrosion. Evaluating these interconnected phenomena across a six-month window provides a necessary diagnostic perspective: a single month captures only isolated values and transient records, whereas six months reveal whether reported performance metrics actually moved or stagnated. A half-year view cannot resolve multi-thousand-hour degradation trajectories or guarantee twenty-year service lifetimes, but it offers sufficient temporal depth to distinguish genuine thermodynamic stabilization from statistical noise and short-lived laboratory anomalies.
